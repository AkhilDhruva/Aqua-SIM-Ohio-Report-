Independent hindcast study · Central Ohio flood of 19–20 August 2026

What a rainfall flood model can and cannot be trusted to decide

A blind reconstruction of the Franklin and Licking County flood, tested against what actually happened — and a specific, quantified limit on using regional flood screening for road-closure decisions.

Prepared 23 August 2026 Domain Greater Columbus & western Licking County Forcing NOAA MRMS radar QPE Terrain USGS 3DEP 1 m LiDAR Status Research — not operationally validated

What this document is. An independent technical study produced with a research flood simulator. It is not a product of, endorsed by, or reviewed by ODOT, the National Weather Service, USGS, or any Ohio agency. Observed impacts are drawn from public reporting and public data; several timestamps are approximate or inferred and are labelled as such. Nothing here should be used as the basis of an operational decision without agency review and independent verification.

Bottom line

  • Holds up

    Regional screening works. Driven only by published rainfall and public terrain — with predictions frozen before any flood report was consulted — the model placed 16 of 17 documented flood locations among its deepest water. Against a matched random baseline that outcome would occur by chance about 3–7% of the time.

  • Does not hold up

    Road-level closure calls from a 60 m grid are unsafe. At the I-70 crossing of the South Fork Licking River, the coarse model declares the interstate impassable while its own water surface sits 1.77 m below the actual pavement. The flooding it sees is the channel, not the roadway.

  • Retracted

    The apparent 11.6-hour warning on the I-70 closure does not survive scrutiny. The model's early signal arrived before the upstream river gauge had even peaked, so it cannot be the flood wave that closed the road. We report it as a false positive, not a lead time.

  • Operationally useful

    The observed routing lag is the real warning asset. The South Fork crest took roughly 28 hours to travel from Kirkersville to the Buckeye Lake reach. That lag — measured, not modelled — is genuine, actionable lead time for the I-70 corridor.

01

What was tested, and how the test was kept honest

Cause data in, predictions frozen, impacts gathered only afterwards

The simulator was given two things and nothing else: the rainfall that fell, and the shape of the ground. Rainfall came from NOAA's Multi-Radar Multi-Sensor system — both the low-latency radar-only product that would have been available in real time, and the gauge-corrected retrospective product. Terrain came from USGS 3DEP, at 1 m resolution over the study corridors.

The order of operations is what makes the result meaningful. Sensor locations were placed automatically by the terrain itself, not by hand. The predictions were then sealed under content-derived identifiers. Only after that were the seventeen documented flood locations collected from public reporting and scored by a script whose thresholds were fixed in advance. No rainfall, roughness, terrain, or threshold was ever adjusted to improve agreement with a known outcome.

Rainfall was checked against gauges over matched accumulation windows and the difference reported rather than corrected. Over the storm window the retrospective product landed within 4% of the Buckeye Lake observation and inside the spread of the Pataskala gauges; the radar-only product ran materially low in this warm-rain convective event, which is a real limitation of that feed and is carried forward as such.

02

The finding that matters for infrastructure

A 60-metre cell cannot tell a roadway from the stream beneath it

A regional screening grid divides the landscape into 60-metre squares and gives each square a single ground elevation. Where a highway crosses a stream, that one square contains the carriageway, the embankment, and the channel bed all at once. Whichever elevation the grid adopts becomes the level at which "the road" floods in the model.

At the I-70 crossing of the South Fork Licking River, the grid adopted an elevation of 267.45 m — roughly halfway between the channel invert at 265.57 m and the true carriageway at 269.51 m. The consequence is direct and measurable.

This is not a single unlucky location. Applying the same measurement across twelve monitored corridor points shows the error runs in both directions, which matters: a coarse grid can invent a closure, and it can also miss one.

03

Why the early I-70 signal was not a warning

A flood wave cannot arrive downstream before it peaks upstream

The screening run crossed its impassability threshold at the I-70 corridor at 10:25Z on 20 August, roughly eleven and a half hours before ODOT closed the interstate. Taken at face value that looks like a substantial early warning. It is not, and the reason is visible in the observed river record rather than in the model.

The South Fork Licking River gauge upstream at Kirkersville did not peak until 13:00Z — two and a half hours after the model's signal. The downstream response in the Buckeye Lake reach did not peak until the following afternoon, a routing lag of about twenty-eight hours. Water that closed I-70 had not yet been delivered to the upstream gauge when the model announced the road was impassable.

We are withdrawing the eleven-and-a-half-hour lead time. The model was not early about the closure; it was reporting a different kind of water, in a place its grid could not resolve.

Formal retraction — recorded in the study's determination file

04

What the model could not do at all

Multi-day river routing is outside its represented physics

Extending the simulation to fifty-six hours to reach for the observed crest exposed a structural defect rather than a tuning problem. After the rainfall stops, water at the I-70 corridor recedes at a constant rate that exactly matches the model's drainage parameter, instead of rising toward the crest that actually arrived a day later.

Two causes, both identified before any comparison to the observed crest. The model's storm-drain term removes water uniformly across the domain, so it drains routed river water that should be travelling downstream. And nothing in the configuration represents lake storage, dam or spillway operation, baseflow, or inflow entering from beyond the domain boundary. The twenty-eight-hour lag through the Buckeye Lake system is precisely the process the configuration omits.

This is reported as a discovered defect. A sensitivity run isolating the drainage term is queued, and it will quantify the term's contribution rather than fix the outcome.

05

What this means for Ohio infrastructure decisions

Four recommendations that follow directly from the measurements above

Recommendation 01

Use regional flood screening for staging, not for closures

The 60-metre screen reliably identifies which corridors and neighbourhoods will be in trouble, which is exactly what resource pre-positioning, crew staging, and shelter activation need. It should not be permitted to generate road-open or road-closed guidance, in either direction, at any crossing.

Recommendation 02

Tie closure thresholds to surveyed pavement elevations

Every point where the model's ground elevation and the surveyed carriageway diverge by more than half a metre is a place where automated guidance will be wrong. The fix is inexpensive: pair each flood-prone crossing with its surveyed pavement elevation and evaluate the modelled water surface against that number, not against a grid cell.

Recommendation 03

Treat upstream gauges as the warning instrument for the I-70 corridor

The measured twenty-eight-hour travel time between the Kirkersville reach and the Buckeye Lake reach is real lead time available today, from instruments already in the ground. For this corridor a gauge-threshold rule offers warning that no rainfall model in this study matched.

Recommendation 04

Verify culvert and bridge openings before trusting fine-scale results

Detecting stream crossings automatically from elevation data reached 91–96% precision against 1 m LiDAR across three corridors, which is useful but not sufficient. A crossing wrongly modelled as solid embankment creates a dam that does not exist — and a high-resolution model can be confidently wrong in a way a coarse one is not.

06

Limitations

Stated plainly, because they bound every claim above

No false-alarm rate. Public reporting records what flooded, never what stayed dry. Without an authoritative record of roads that remained open, the model's tendency to over-predict cannot be quantified — only its detection can.

River observations are a proxy. Direct USGS endpoints were unreachable from the study environment. River timings come from National Water Model analysis fields that assimilate live USGS gauges — closely anchored to observation, but not raw observation, and carrying discharge only. No stage comparison, and therefore no stage error statistic, is possible here.

Several impact timestamps are approximate. Closure and rescue times come from press accounts, some phrased as "around" a given hour, and at least one closure start is inferred rather than stated. Two independently reported accounts of the I-70 closure time disagree by roughly ninety minutes.

Fine-scale simulations are still running. The corridor models at 6–8 m will confirm the mechanism identified here by showing directly whether carriageway cells wet during the storm. The direction of the finding does not depend on them — it rests on surveyed elevations and observed gauge timing — but the dynamic confirmation is not yet complete.

Buildings are not represented. Building footprint sources were unreachable from the study environment, so urban flow obstruction in Franklinton is modelled by terrain alone.

One methodological change followed exposure to outcome data. The simulation window was extended after the observed crest timing was known. It is logged as such in the study record. All forcing, geometry, roughness, and thresholds were fixed beforehand and never altered.
